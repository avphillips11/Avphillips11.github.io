In August I was promoted into a role that made AI governance part of my job instead of a side project. Leadership was already asking a fair question: what is all this AI actually costing us, and is it worth it?

This is what I built in the three months after that, mostly with the same AI team I'd already built for my GRC work.

Section I

The Problem

Claude had gone from a pilot to something most of the company used regularly. But nobody could answer some basic questions:

  • Who uses what, and which models?
  • What does it cost, by person and by department?
  • Which model should be the default for which kind of work?
  • What are people allowed to put into these tools?
  • When cost becomes a reason to approve or deny access, who decides?

I didn't want the answer to be "cut access." The people getting the most out of AI are often the ones spending the most. My goal was to make cost visible, put sensible defaults in place, and let people keep doing good work.

Section II

What I Built

A real baseline. I pulled usage and modeled spend from the enterprise analytics data and broke it down by surface (Chat, Code, Cowork), model and department. The first monthly baseline query came back about 5.6x too high. A plausibility check caught it before it went anywhere: we were reading the wrong column. I'd rather catch that myself than have Finance catch it for me.

Defaults instead of rules. Most of the cost lever turned out to be model mix, not usage. We changed the default models by surface, scheduled a legacy model's retirement, and put our most expensive model behind a short request workflow with manager and finance approval. Cost per million tokens fell 36–39% in about two months, almost all of it from usage moving to a newer, cheaper model. We made it the default. The lower price was the vendor's, and the report to leadership split the credit that way.

Visibility people can use. Every user now gets a weekly personal chat message with their own usage and modeled spend by product and model, plus how they compare to their department's median. I built in a few deliberate limits:

  • Comparisons only show for groups of five or more, and fall back to company-wide when a group is too small.
  • No names and no leaderboards.
  • Every dollar figure carries a "this is modeled, not billed" note.
  • It reads as a receipt, not a performance review.

For the do-it-yourself reporting I chose a metadata-only data source over one that reads conversation content. That was a privacy decision.

Policy that matches how people work. I drafted a new AI Acceptable Use Policy and reworked the companion AI lifecycle policy. That took twelve rounds of decisions in one day and an adversarial review that failed the first draft. The principle I pushed hardest for: usage data should help people use AI well. It should never be used to judge them.

Two gates, not one. Cost is the department budget owner's call. Security, governance and privacy are the governance committee's. Neither substitutes for the other. I found program documents sending cost approvals to the committee and fixed them.

Getting the regulatory scope right. I mapped our AI documentation to what each applicable standard actually requires, and cut work that no standard asked for. While doing that we checked our control citations against the licensed standard and found some that don't exist. Now the team has a rule: no clause number unless it's been checked against the real text.

Section III

How the AI Team Works

I run a team of 16 AI agents in Claude Code, including an orchestrator that routes the work. Each one has a role, a persona and a model tier. The orchestrator never does the work itself. I approve everything that leaves the team.

What changed this quarter is mostly how the team governs itself:

I
Delegated approval with no discretion

The orchestrator can pass along my approval only when I've explicitly approved that specific action, and each one gets an audit line. Hiring new agents and posting in public cross-team channels stay with me.

II
Model tiers enforced at dispatch

We found that subagents were quietly inheriting the orchestrator's (more expensive) model. Now every dispatch names its model, and an end-of-session check flags any mismatch.

III
Measured cost, not estimated

A script prices every session from the transcripts. When we find a data gap, I note it instead of adjusting the numbers.

IV
Version control on the workspace

Reviewers check changes against a diff, not against the author's description.

V
An adversarial QA agent

Its verdicts are written to disk. This quarter it rejected a policy draft, a new skill and an executive tracker before each one passed.

VI
Expiration dates on facts

Any claim about a live system carries a re-check date based on what it would cost if it went stale.

The team now has 11 reusable skills. The newest one runs the full quarterly risk committee cycle: data pull, register updates, deck, minutes and follow-ups, with every write gated.

Section IV

What I Learned

I
A check that compares a document to itself proves nothing.

Check against the inputs.

II
A failed search is evidence about the search, not about the world.

We once flagged real quotes as "fabricated" because a search missed them.

III
Plausible citations survive review.

Verify against the source or say you didn't.

IV
Lower-cost defaults are a better first lever than restrictions.
V
When a number changes, label the change.

Don't quietly swap it.

VI
Report the number that's true.

My hours-saved multiplier was about 9x when I audited it in August and ~7.9x at the last full dashboard refresh. For September to November it's about 4x, and that stretch hasn't been audited yet. The work shifted from one-time builds to running a program. That's what should happen, so I report it.
